## Introduction

How many different ways can a robot arm move? This seemingly simple question leads us to one of the most fundamental concepts in robotics: degrees of freedom (DOF). Degrees of freedom tell us how many independent movements a mechanical system can make—and understanding this concept is essential for designing robots that can reach the positions and orientations needed to complete their tasks.

Think of your own arm. You can move your shoulder in multiple directions, bend your elbow, rotate your forearm, and move your wrist. Each of these independent movements represents a degree of freedom. A humanoid robot's arm works similarly, with joints that allow specific types of motion combined into a kinematic chain—a series of rigid bodies connected by joints.

In this chapter, we will explore how to count and classify degrees of freedom, understand different joint types, and analyze kinematic chains that form the skeleton of robotic systems.

## Theory Content

### Section 1: What Are Degrees of Freedom?

**Degrees of freedom (DOF)** represent the number of independent parameters needed to completely specify the position and orientation of a system. In robotics, DOF determines what positions and orientations a robot can achieve.

**Free Body in Space**: A rigid body floating freely in 3D space has 6 DOF:
- 3 translational (movement along X, Y, Z axes)
- 3 rotational (rotation around X, Y, Z axes)

:::note Key Concept
The DOF of a robot determines its workspace—the set of all positions and orientations its end effector can reach. More DOF generally means more flexibility, but also more complexity.
:::

**DOF in Robotic Systems**:
- A simple gripper that only opens/closes: 1 DOF
- A planar robot arm moving in 2D: typically 2-3 DOF
- A typical industrial robot arm: 6 DOF
- A humanoid robot hand: 20+ DOF

### Section 2: Joint Types

Joints are the connections between rigid links that allow relative motion. Different joint types provide different types of motion.

**Revolute Joint (R)**: Allows rotation around a single axis. Like a door hinge.
- Provides: 1 DOF (rotation)
- Most common joint type in robotics
- Example: Elbow joint, robot arm joints

**Prismatic Joint (P)**: Allows linear sliding along an axis. Like a drawer.
- Provides: 1 DOF (translation)
- Used in linear actuators, some robot bases
- Example: Telescoping mechanisms

**Spherical Joint (S)**: Allows rotation around all three axes. Like a ball-and-socket.
- Provides: 3 DOF (three rotations)
- Difficult to actuate directly
- Example: Human hip joint

**Cylindrical Joint (C)**: Combines rotation and translation along the same axis.
- Provides: 2 DOF (one rotation + one translation)

**Universal Joint (U)**: Two perpendicular revolute joints.
- Provides: 2 DOF (two perpendicular rotations)
- Example: Car drive shaft

| Joint Type | Symbol | DOF | Motion Type |
|------------|--------|-----|-------------|
| Revolute | R | 1 | Rotation |
| Prismatic | P | 1 | Translation |
| Helical | H | 1 | Coupled rotation-translation |
| Cylindrical | C | 2 | Independent rotation + translation |
| Universal | U | 2 | Two perpendicular rotations |
| Spherical | S | 3 | Three rotations |

### Section 3: Kinematic Chains

A **kinematic chain** is a series of rigid links connected by joints. The configuration of a kinematic chain determines what the robot can do.

**Open vs. Closed Chains**:

**Open kinematic chain (serial chain)**: Links form a single path from base to end effector.
- Example: Most robot arms
- Easy to analyze and control
- Each joint's motion directly affects the end effector

**Closed kinematic chain (parallel chain)**: Links form one or more loops.
- Example: Delta robot, Stewart platform
- More rigid and precise
- More complex kinematics

**The Grübler-Kutzbach Formula**:

For calculating DOF of a planar mechanism:
$$DOF = 3(n-1) - 2j_1 - j_2$$

Where:
- n = number of links (including ground)
- j₁ = number of 1-DOF joints
- j₂ = number of 2-DOF joints

For spatial (3D) mechanisms:
$$DOF = 6(n-1) - 5j_1 - 4j_2 - 3j_3 - 2j_4 - j_5$$

### Section 4: Common Robot Configurations

Different robot configurations suit different tasks:

**Cartesian (PPP)**: Three prismatic joints along X, Y, Z.
- Simple kinematics
- Large, boxy workspace
- Example: 3D printers, CNC machines

**Cylindrical (RPP)**: Revolute + two prismatic joints.
- Cylindrical workspace
- Good for pick-and-place

**Spherical (RRP)**: Two revolute + one prismatic joint.
- Spherical workspace segment
- Used in some welding robots

**Articulated (RRR...R)**: All revolute joints.
- Most common industrial robot type
- Complex kinematics but flexible workspace
- Example: Most 6-DOF robot arms

**SCARA (RRP)**: Selective Compliance Assembly Robot Arm.
- Good for assembly tasks
- Stiff vertically, compliant horizontally

```python
# Lab 2.1: Degrees of Freedom and Kinematic Chains
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.patches import Circle, Rectangle, FancyArrow

print("=== Degrees of Freedom Exploration ===\n")

# Part 1: DOF Calculator using Grübler formula
def calculate_dof_planar(num_links, joints_1dof, joints_2dof=0):
    """Calculate DOF for planar mechanism using Grübler formula"""
    # n includes ground link
    dof = 3 * (num_links - 1) - 2 * joints_1dof - joints_2dof
    return dof

# Example mechanisms
mechanisms = [
    {"name": "Four-bar linkage", "links": 4, "j1": 4, "j2": 0},
    {"name": "Slider-crank", "links": 4, "j1": 3, "j2": 1},
    {"name": "Simple pendulum", "links": 2, "j1": 1, "j2": 0},
    {"name": "3-DOF planar arm", "links": 4, "j1": 3, "j2": 0},
]

print("DOF Calculations for Common Mechanisms:")
print("-" * 50)
for mech in mechanisms:
    dof = calculate_dof_planar(mech["links"], mech["j1"], mech["j2"])
    print(f"{mech['name']:20} Links={mech['links']}, J1={mech['j1']}, J2={mech['j2']} → DOF={dof}")
```

```python
# Part 3: 3-DOF Planar Arm Simulation

class PlanarArm:
    def __init__(self, link_lengths):
        self.L = link_lengths
        self.num_joints = len(link_lengths)

    def forward_kinematics(self, joint_angles):
        """Compute positions of all joints given joint angles"""
        positions = [(0, 0)]  # Base position
        cumulative_angle = 0

        for i, (L, theta) in enumerate(zip(self.L, joint_angles)):
            cumulative_angle += theta
            x = positions[-1][0] + L * np.cos(cumulative_angle)
            y = positions[-1][1] + L * np.sin(cumulative_angle)
            positions.append((x, y))

        return positions

    def visualize(self, joint_angles, ax=None, color='blue'):
        if ax is None:
            fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 8))

        positions = self.forward_kinematics(joint_angles)

        # Draw links
        for i in range(len(positions)-1):
            ax.plot([positions[i][0], positions[i+1][0]],
                   [positions[i][1], positions[i+1][1]],
                   '-', linewidth=6, color=color, alpha=0.7)

        # Draw joints
        for i, pos in enumerate(positions):
            size = 12 if i == 0 else 10
            ax.plot(pos[0], pos[1], 'ko', markersize=size)

        # Mark end effector
        ax.plot(positions[-1][0], positions[-1][1], 'r*', markersize=15)

        return positions[-1]

# Create a 3-DOF arm
arm = PlanarArm([1.0, 0.8, 0.5])  # Link lengths

# Visualize different configurations
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(12, 12))

configurations = [
    ([0, 0, 0], "All joints at 0°"),
    ([np.pi/4, 0, 0], "Joint 1 at 45°"),
    ([np.pi/4, np.pi/4, 0], "Joints 1,2 at 45°"),
    ([np.pi/4, np.pi/4, -np.pi/2], "Joints 1,2 at 45°, 3 at -90°"),
]

for ax, (angles, title) in zip(axes.flat, configurations):
    end_pos = arm.visualize(angles, ax)
    ax.set_xlim(-2.5, 2.5)
    ax.set_ylim(-2.5, 2.5)
    ax.set_aspect('equal')
    ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
    ax.set_title(f'{title}\nEnd effector: ({end_pos[0]:.2f}, {end_pos[1]:.2f})')
    ax.axhline(y=0, color='k', linewidth=0.5)
    ax.axvline(x=0, color='k', linewidth=0.5)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

print(f"\n3-DOF Arm Properties:")
print(f"  Total link length: {sum(arm.L):.1f} units")
print(f"  Maximum reach: {sum(arm.L):.1f} units")
print(f"  Degrees of freedom: {arm.num_joints}")
```

## Case Study: The KUKA LBR iiwa 7 DOF Arm

### Background

Most industrial robots have 6 DOF—exactly enough to position and orient an end effector arbitrarily in 3D space. KUKA's LBR iiwa (intelligent industrial work assistant) has 7 DOF, making it "kinematically redundant." Why add an extra joint?

### Implementation

The LBR iiwa's 7 revolute joints provide:
- 6 DOF for arbitrary end effector pose
- 1 additional DOF for reconfiguring the arm's posture

This redundancy allows the robot to:
- Avoid obstacles while maintaining end effector position
- Optimize for minimal energy or maximum manipulability
- Work in confined spaces by "elbowing" around obstacles

### Results

The 7th DOF enables applications not possible with 6 DOF arms:
- Working alongside humans with better obstacle avoidance
- Reaching around corners in assembly tasks
- Maintaining optimal arm configuration throughout trajectories

### Lessons Learned

1. **More DOF = more capability but more complexity**: 7 DOF requires more sophisticated control
2. **Redundancy enables optimization**: Extra DOF can optimize secondary objectives
3. **Human-robot collaboration benefits**: Redundancy helps navigate shared workspaces
4. **Application-specific design**: Not all tasks need 7 DOF; choose based on requirements

## Summary

Degrees of freedom and kinematic chains form the foundation of robot mechanical design:

- **DOF** determines what positions and orientations a robot can achieve
- **Joint types** (revolute, prismatic, spherical, etc.) provide different motion capabilities
- **Kinematic chains** connect joints and links into functional mechanisms
- **The Grübler formula** helps calculate DOF for complex mechanisms
- **Robot configurations** (Cartesian, articulated, SCARA, etc.) suit different applications
- **Redundancy** (extra DOF) enables additional capabilities like obstacle avoidance

## Quiz

### Question 1

How many DOF does a rigid body have when floating freely in 3D space?

A) 3
B) 4
C) 6
D) 9

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**C**: A rigid body in 3D space has 6 DOF—3 translational (X, Y, Z position) and 3 rotational (rotation around X, Y, Z axes).
</details>

### Question 2

Which joint type provides exactly 1 DOF of rotational motion?

A) Prismatic
B) Revolute
C) Spherical
D) Universal

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**B**: A revolute joint provides 1 DOF of rotational motion around a single axis, like a door hinge. A prismatic joint provides 1 DOF of translational motion.
</details>

### Question 3

A typical 6-DOF industrial robot arm uses which configuration?

A) All prismatic joints (PPP-PPP)
B) All revolute joints (articulated)
C) Mixed prismatic and revolute (SCARA)
D) Spherical joints only

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**B**: Most 6-DOF industrial robots are articulated, using all revolute joints. This provides a flexible workspace and is mechanically straightforward to implement.
</details>

### Question 4

What is a "kinematically redundant" robot?

A) A robot with broken joints
B) A robot with more DOF than needed for a task
C) A robot with exactly 6 DOF
D) A robot with less DOF than needed

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**B**: A kinematically redundant robot has more DOF than the minimum needed to achieve a given task. For example, a 7-DOF arm is redundant for 6-DOF tasks, allowing optimization of additional criteria.
</details>

### Question 5

According to the Grübler formula for a planar mechanism with 4 links and 4 single-DOF joints (including the ground link), what is the DOF?

A) 0
B) 1
C) 2
D) 3

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>
**B**: DOF = 3(n-1) - 2j₁ = 3(4-1) - 2(4) = 9 - 8 = 1. A four-bar linkage has 1 DOF.
</details>
